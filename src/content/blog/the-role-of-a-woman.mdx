> _“The desire and pursuit of the whole is called love.”_ — Plato

In this hyper-connected country of 6 billion people called the Internet, as it gets more crowded, it becomes harder to find authentic and contextual advice on dating and relationships. Most gurus and content creators are least bothered about adding solid value. They want to hack your brain for a reaction — making you angry at the opposite gender, making you believe you’re losing your freedom, and making you feel weak for seeking love.

The most annoying of them all are the love gurus who preach “self-love.” They all start with the same line, “love yourself first,” and tell you to be the only person responsible for your happiness. That’s fantastic advice for people depending on their relationships for inner stability or survival. But I don’t believe most normal people benefit from it.

I fell for it too. When I was growing up, my parents were often posted to different places for work, so I never had a strong example of what dating and relationships look like. The internet made me believe this was a major childhood dysfunction, and that the fix was to love myself absolutely batshit crazy. But compared to my friend, who lost his father at a young age, was my situation really that dysfunctional? I don’t think so.

Anyway, I took the internet’s advice to heart and went all in on self-love and hyper-independence. And still, I never felt the sense of completeness in a relationship, which was the promise made by these gurus.

It was only after I met my very loving, caring, and sweet girlfriend that I understood why. I started feeling whole with her — a sense of expanding consciousness and unlocking parts of my psyche, which simply cannot be done without her. And this wholeness feels different from neediness.

As my consciousness expanded, I started noticing things I’d never paid attention to: the beauty in small things, like a photo we took inside a booth or a dinner made with care. I became more empathetic and less judgemental. I got better at saying how I feel. And the softness I once saw as weakness is now something I can reach for without thinking. 

Now I’m more convinced than ever that this is the whole point of a romantic relationship: finding the person who makes you feel whole. Not status, money, or fame, but someone who lets you experience parts of yourself you never knew existed.

Carl Jung saw something similar. He believed that in the second half of life, a man should integrate the feminine and a woman the masculine. If you meet the right person, your psyche starts pushing for that fuller, more balanced development, making you more whole. But you can’t get there alone. As Jung put it, “The unrelated human being lacks wholeness.” You need someone to explore with.

From a stranger on the internet, my anti-love-guru advice: find someone who expands your consciousness and makes you feel whole. Of course, you can lead a happy life without them, but it’s just that you can’t lead a whole life.
